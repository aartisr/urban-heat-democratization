# City Onboarding and Community Partnership

## A city is more than a boundary file

The software can begin onboarding with a usable GeoJSON boundary. A meaningful urban-heat program cannot. It needs local relationships, appropriate evidence, public purpose, and a clear agreement about who benefits from the work.

The platform’s incremental onboarding design makes technical readiness visible as it grows. The partnership model should do the same for civic readiness.

## Technical readiness ladder

| Stage | What is available | What it supports | What it cannot support yet |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Boundary | A usable city or study-area geometry | Orientation and basic onboarding | Heat conclusions |
| 2. Local layers | Thermal and contextual inputs with provenance | Exploratory layer inspection | Validated intervention claims |
| 3. Study artifacts | Documented overlays and method settings | Structured local analysis | Procurement-ready decisions |
| 4. Cost and feasibility | Local quantities, prices, constraints, maintenance inputs | Serious planning review | Guaranteed outcomes |
| 5. Evaluation | Baseline, implementation data, and outcome monitoring | Learning and accountability | Universal causal proof |

Boston currently occupies a stronger position than other cities because it is the bundled study case with local boundary and overlay artifacts. It still does not have a complete city-specific cost and benefit model in this repository.

## Partnership before deployment

Before presenting a new city analysis publicly, establish:

- a named public-interest purpose;
- local partners who can interpret, challenge, and use the work;
- data provenance, license, privacy, retention, and access expectations;
- an accessibility and language plan for public-facing materials;
- a process for residents to identify omissions or harms;
- an escalation path when evidence conflicts with lived experience;
- a clear owner for maintenance and update decisions.

Community knowledge is not a decorative validation step. It can identify missing shade routes, informal cooling spaces, unsafe crossings, construction conditions, service gaps, or displacement risks that a spatial model does not see.

## Practical onboarding workflow

1. Define the decision or learning question with local partners.
2. Establish the geographic scope and provide a usable boundary.
3. Inventory available layers, their provenance, dates, restrictions, and gaps.
4. Register inputs and document the method configuration.
5. Review preliminary patterns with people who know the area.
6. Use benchmark scenarios only as prompts for further study.
7. Commission the appropriate technical, public-health, and community review before formal commitments.
8. Publish a plain-language account of inputs, limitations, decisions, and subsequent outcomes where safe and appropriate.

## A practical test of success

An onboarding is successful when a local group can answer: What did we put into this system? What did it show? What did it miss? Who reviewed it? What will happen because of it? If those answers are unavailable, the city is not ready for high-stakes use—regardless of how polished the map appears.
