# A Civic Starter Guide to Urban Heat Evidence

You do not need to be a data scientist to use this workspace well. You need a
clear local question, a willingness to examine the evidence, and people who
know the place.

## Begin with one decision

Ask a question a public group could actually act on. For example: *Which
walking routes, public spaces, or neighborhoods should we investigate first
for cooling access during extreme heat?* Do not begin by asking the map to
declare a winner or diagnose a community.

## Use the platform in four moves

1. **See the pattern.** Open a city and look at the boundary, heat-related
   layers, and cooling-access context. Notice what is shown before deciding
   what it means.
2. **Read the evidence label.** Every useful claim should identify its source,
   date, scale, and limitation. A derived pattern is a prompt for local
   investigation—not a verdict about residents or a promise of cooling.
3. **Add local knowledge.** Ask residents, public-health teams, service
   providers, planners, and accessibility advocates what the map misses:
   shade timing, unsafe crossings, indoor conditions, maintenance, access, or
   places people actually use.
4. **Choose the next evidence step.** This may be a site visit, an updated
   local dataset, a language-accessible meeting, an engineering review, or a
   small monitored pilot. Only then should a public body consider a decision.

## What the pages are for

| Page | Use it for | Do not use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cities** | Seeing what evidence a city currently has and starting a new city with a preconfigured starter. | Assuming every city has the same level of local data. |
| **City atlas** | Inspecting supplied layers, sources, and readiness. | Inferring a property-level or personal heat risk. |
| **Scenarios** | Comparing stated what-if assumptions and budget tradeoffs. | Predicting a guaranteed temperature, health, or cost outcome. |
| **Robustness lab** | Learning how network assumptions affect a modeled signal. | Treating the synthetic lab as a live city measurement. |
| **Exports and run history** | Sharing the inputs, choices, and records behind a discussion. | Replacing public review or local validation. |

## Starting a new city without a technical handoff

On the **Cities** page, choose a preconfigured city starter. It fills in the
repeatable identity and study context so the team can focus on the first local
requirement: a usable GeoJSON boundary. The starter intentionally marks the
city as boundary-ready only after that file is supplied. Thermal layers,
spectral overlays, costs, and local findings remain unregistered until they
are actually available and reviewed.

That distinction protects the public from a polished but unsupported result.
For the complete technical path, see [City Onboarding Recipes](../CITY_ONBOARDING_RECIPES.md).

## A good meeting ends with an accountable next step

Before closing a discussion, record five things: the question, the evidence
used, what is uncertain, who needs to review it, and the next action. Publish
that record in accessible language whenever it is safe to do so. This makes
the work more useful to a resident, an educator, and a public agency at the
same time.

## The standard

The platform can help make a heat question visible and inspectable. It cannot
replace consent, field knowledge, public-health expertise, engineering,
community governance, or democratic decision-making. Those are not obstacles
to the analysis; they are what make it responsible.
